# AI writing tropes

This catalog tracks common model-generated patterns. It is distilled from
[tropes.fyi](https://tropes.fyi/tropes-md) by Ossama Chaib and its
[Writing Whip](https://tropes.fyi/whip), reviewed 2026-08-23.

These patterns are diagnostic, not forbidden grammar. A single use can be
natural. Repetition, clustering, and use without purpose make prose feel
generated.

## Composition and response behavior

### Reasoning leak

The result narrates what the writer is deciding, planning, or trying to clarify.
Do the work and give the result.

### Premise stacking

Evidence and qualifications imply the point several times before the text states
it. State the point first, then give the evidence it needs.

### Preamble

The opening announces the answer, names the shape of what follows, or paraphrases
the prompt. Lead with the answer itself.

### Compulsive counting

The prose boasts about its structure with "five things" or "for two reasons."
Use a list if the content is a list. Mention the count only when it matters.

### Belaboring the unnecessary

A minor claim comes with a defense against an objection nobody raised. State it
and continue.

### Tie-back

The ending loops back to the prompt or repeats the answer. Stop when the answer
is complete.

### Fractal summaries

The text previews, delivers, and recaps the same point at section and document
level. Keep the one version the reader needs.

### One-point dilution

Several paragraphs restate one argument without new evidence or reasoning. Make
the point once and use the best support.

### Content duplication

A section or paragraph repeats content that already appeared. Delete the copy.

### Never-ending conclusion

The ending adds clauses, advice, and another summary after the point has landed.
End on the last necessary sentence.

## Sentence and paragraph patterns

### Negative parallelism

"It is not X. It is Y" frames an ordinary point as a reveal. State Y directly.
This includes "not because X, but because Y" and "does not just X, it Y."

### Negative countdown

"Not X. Not Y. Just Z" delays the actual claim for dramatic effect. State Z.

### Self-answered question

"The result? Devastating." asks a question nobody asked and answers it for
emphasis. Turn it into a statement.

### Short punchy fragments

Clusters of fragments or tiny standalone paragraphs manufacture emphasis:
"Openly. In a book. As a priest." Use complete sentences and natural rhythm.

### Rule-of-three habit

Repeated tricolons make prose sound generated. Keep a useful tricolon, but vary
the size and shape of nearby lists.

### Anaphora abuse

Several sentences begin with the same words. Combine related claims or vary the
structure.

### Excessive enumeration

Paragraphs beginning "First," "Second," and "Third" disguise a list as prose.
Write a real list or remove the numbering.

### False range

"From X to Y" implies a scale that does not exist. List the items unless they
have a real continuum and meaningful middle.

### Superficial analysis

A trailing participle phrase gestures at significance: "highlighting its
importance" or "reflecting broader trends." Make the analytical claim specific
or delete the phrase.

### Comma-clipped tail

A short phrase hangs after a comma to give the sentence a sideways finish. Land
the point in a full clause or remove the tail.

## Tone and claims

### Grandiose stakes

The prose turns a local claim into a transformation of an industry, society, or
the future. Match the claim to the evidence and actual scale.

### Invented concept label

A compound such as "supervision paradox" or "acceleration trap" substitutes a
name for an argument. Define a useful new term or describe the behavior plainly.

### Magic adverb

Words such as "quietly," "deeply," "fundamentally," "remarkably," and
"arguably" lend significance without evidence. State the property or cut the
adverb.

### Vague attribution

"Experts," "observers," and "industry reports" hide the source and can inflate
one opinion into consensus. Name and cite the source or remove the claim.

### Appeal to familiarity

"Famously," "notoriously," "a classic," and "as we all know" borrow authority
from assumed consensus. Supply evidence instead.

### Quotable one-liner

A standalone sentence aims to sound extractable but carries little information.
Replace slide bait with a concrete claim.

### Promotional language

The prose sells instead of describes, using words such as "seamless,"
"all-in-one," "unprecedented," or "elevates." Name capabilities and limits.
Marketing copy can persuade, but it still needs evidence and audience fit.

### False suspense

"Here's the kicker," "here's what most people miss," and "but here's the catch"
promise a reveal. Give the point without the drumroll.

### Pedagogical reflex

"Let's break this down" and "think of it as" assume a teacher-student relation
that the task did not request. Explain at the reader's level without announcing
the lesson.

### False vulnerability

A polished confession performs honesty without taking a specific risk. Keep
personal admissions concrete and relevant.

### Collaborative voice drift

The text changes "I" to "we" or invents a shared "we" without an authorial
reason. Preserve the author's person and point of view.

### Formulaic optimism

"Despite these challenges" mentions a problem only to dismiss it in an upbeat
ending. Examine the problem or leave it out.

## Word choice

### Synonym cycling

One thing receives several names for variety. Pick the established term and
repeat it.

### Ornate stock nouns

"Tapestry," "landscape," "paradigm," "synergy," and vague uses of "ecosystem"
or "framework" make ordinary subjects sound grand. Use the concrete noun.
"Load-bearing" and "gated" also need their literal or technical meaning.

### Pompous copula

"Serves as," "stands as," "marks," or "represents" replaces a plain "is."
Use the simple verb unless the distinction is real.

### Model vocabulary

"Delve," "certainly," "utilize," "leverage" as a verb, "robust," "streamline,"
and "harness" recur in generated prose. Replace them when a plainer word carries
the same meaning.

### Location metaphor

"Where the complexity actually lives" turns a direct source or cause into a
place. Name the component, cause, or owner.

### Self-echo

A distinctive word or phrase returns as if it were a planned callback. Keep the
callback only if it adds meaning and sounds intentional.

### Forced figurative language

A simile or metaphor exists to sound clever rather than clarify. This includes
repurposing a word from the prompt as an unrelated metaphor. Use literal
language or one accurate image.

## Formatting

### Em-dash addiction

Repeated em dashes create dramatic pauses and parenthetical pivots. Use periods,
commas, or parentheses where they fit. A few natural em dashes are not a defect.

### Wh-word headings

Repeated headings beginning "What," "Where," or "Why" reveal a template. Name
the section's subject directly.

### Title case headings

Capitalizing every important word makes ordinary documentation look packaged.
Use sentence case unless the house style requires title case.

### Bold-first bullets

Every bullet begins with a bold label, whether or not labels aid scanning. Use
plain bullets by default and labels only for real categories.

### Unicode decoration

Arrows, icons, and smart punctuation decorate plain technical text. Use the
project's typography. In source-oriented documentation, prefer straight quotes
and `->` where an arrow is necessary.

### Signposted conclusion

"In conclusion," "to sum up," and "in summary" announce an ending the reader can
already see. Begin the final claim directly, or omit a redundant conclusion.

## Analogy and imagery

### Rapid-fire historical analogies

A list of companies or technological shifts creates borrowed authority without
analysis. Use one relevant example and explain the comparison.

### "Imagine a world"

An invitation to picture a perfect future sells the premise before supporting
it. Describe the likely outcome and its basis.

### Repeated metaphor

One image appears in every section until it controls the argument. Use it once,
then return to literal terms.
